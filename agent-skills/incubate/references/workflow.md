# Discovery workflow

Use the section for the current mode. Stop when its completion criterion is met.

## Initial mapping

Build the first map from what the user has already said before asking more questions.

1. Write a provisional domain frame: motives, hoped-for outcomes or experiences, observed problems when present, ideas already mentioned, people involved, constraints, and assumptions.
2. Cluster the material by motive, outcome, and causal connection. A shared tool, audience, or body of information is weak evidence that two clusters belong together.
3. Name candidate threads without embedding a particular approach in the name.
4. Derive a few decision-changing inquiries. Record dependencies only when one answer genuinely changes how another question should be asked.
5. Recommend the first ready inquiry based on importance to the user, uncertainty, cost of learning, and current energy.

Initial mapping is complete when `DOMAIN.md` gives a faithful provisional frame, `MAP.md` separates the known threads and bets, and at least one inquiry is ready or the exact blocker is recorded.

## Unpack

Use unpacking when the user's language mixes motives, outcomes, problems, and approaches.

- Ask what makes the project appealing even if someone else has already done it.
- Translate an approach into the outcome or experience it is meant to produce.
- Ask for concrete examples when the user claims a problem. Leave the problem absent when none exists.
- Separate distinct people, settings, motives, and outcomes when their success conditions differ.
- Record ideas without promoting them to accepted scope.
- Turn consequential unknowns into assumptions or inquiries.

Unpacking is complete when the active thread has an explicit motive, a hoped-for result or experience, any real problems, and separately labeled ideas and assumptions.

## Explore

Use exploration to widen the option set without choosing a bet.

- Explore different ways to honor the motive or reach the outcome. Options may include practice, study, making, existing tools, configuration, composition, collaboration, service work, publishing, and custom software.
- Vary the project boundary as well as its contents.
- Seek alternatives that make meaningfully different commitments or test different explanations.
- When making is part of the motive, include build paths even if an existing tool already delivers the practical result.
- Keep attractive ideas in parking when they do not affect the current decision.

Exploration is complete when the relevant alternatives differ in a decision-worthy way, express the user's motives fairly, and expose their key assumptions. A long list of possible additions does not satisfy this criterion.

## Research

Write the decision and its evidence threshold before searching. Keep the research question narrow enough to finish now.

Prefer primary sources: official documentation, pricing, terms, standards, source code, original works, first-party demonstrations, and direct tests. Use independent sources for experiential claims that their subjects cannot establish themselves. Record the access date for facts that may change.

For each material claim, label its basis:

- **Documented:** a cited source states it.
- **Tested:** a recorded experiment observed it.
- **Inferred:** evidence supports it, but no source or test establishes it directly.
- **Unknown:** the available evidence does not settle it.

Decide what role existing work plays before comparing it. It may be a substitute, reference, component, benchmark, or competitor. Judge it against the project's motives, intended results, and constraints rather than an imagined feature inventory.

Use only the viability tests that can change the decision:

- For practical utility, check whether an existing approach meets the need with less effort.
- For learning, craft, enjoyment, or expression, check feasibility and whether the chosen path provides the desired experience.
- For distinctiveness, check comparable work and define what must be meaningfully different.
- For external adoption or profit, investigate the relevant audience, alternatives, demand, willingness to pay, distribution, and costs at a depth proportionate to the commitment.

An existing solution does not settle whether to make something when making is part of the return. Intrinsic interest does not settle feasibility when the user has named a hard constraint.

Research is complete when the named decision has enough evidence to make or defer it, every material claim has a basis, and unknowns that could reverse the conclusion remain visible.

## Compare

Use a comparison when alternatives, bets, or boundaries compete for attention.

Choose criteria before scoring or ranking. Derive them from the motives, intended outcomes or experiences, constraints, appetite, and evidence. Include commercial criteria only when commercial success is part of the project. Avoid fake precision. Prefer a short qualitative comparison when the evidence cannot support numbers.

Test whether candidates are independent:

- Can one be worthwhile if the other never exists?
- Do they serve different motives, settings, people, or outcomes?
- Would evidence invalidate one without invalidating the other?
- Could an existing approach satisfy the practical need while the act of making remains worthwhile for another motive?

If the answers point apart, keep separate threads or bets even when they share tools or information.

Comparison is complete when the consequential differences, evidence, and tradeoffs are clear enough for the user to choose what to investigate, defer, or reject next.

## Scope review

Review the boundary at initial mapping, after decision-changing evidence, and when a new idea arrives. Skip ceremonial reviews when nothing material changed.

Check for:

- **False unity:** independent motives or outcomes joined because they share people, tools, or information.
- **Missing coherence:** a narrow version that cannot produce the intended result or worthwhile experience.
- **Misread existing work:** ignoring a good substitute when utility is the goal, or using that substitute to dismiss a learning or making motive.
- **Platform gravity:** reusable infrastructure included before two proven consumers need it.
- **Hidden operator:** setup, curation, moderation, support, or maintenance work with no owner.
- **Wrong viability test:** judging a curiosity project by demand, or a commercial project without checking demand and differentiation.
- **Preference disguised as fact:** a recommendation that depends on taste, learning goals, or desired control.
- **Orphan idea:** a new idea attached to the active bet despite having a different motive, outcome, or success test.

Turn a finding into one proposal with this shape:

> Observation → consequence → proposed change → tradeoff → affected records

Possible changes are split, merge, expand, reduce, reuse, experiment, defer, and stop. Mark the proposal as pending until the user decides. Reversible map organization may reflect the proposed view, but accepted boundaries and priorities remain unchanged.

The review is complete when each material concern has either a proposal, a recorded reason to leave the boundary alone, or a named inquiry.

## Shape

Shape one bet, not the whole domain.

Clarify:

- the motive and what would make the effort worthwhile;
- the intended result or experience, plus any problem it addresses;
- the participants, beneficiaries, or recipients when there are any;
- why this bet fits the motive and intended result;
- the smallest coherent commitment that preserves the reason to do it;
- signs of success, review points, and reasons to stop;
- accepted scope, non-goals, appetite, and important constraints;
- existing approaches and other bets considered, including the role each one plays;
- evidence, dependencies, risks, and questions for the next activity;
- novelty, external adoption, demand, and profit only when they are part of success.

Prefer a coherent commitment over a pile of capabilities. Remove pieces that do not support the motive or intended result. Add a missing step when its absence would hollow out the project.

Shaping is complete when a reviewer can explain why the bet is worth doing, what it commits, what is deliberately absent, how the user will judge it, and what evidence or constraint could still stop it.

## Assess

Assessment is read-only. Report the state supported by the current files:

- active thread and bet;
- accepted decisions and pending proposals;
- strongest evidence and decision-threatening unknowns;
- ready, blocked, and active inquiries;
- readiness against the handoff gate.

Assessment is complete when the report identifies the next decision without changing state or turning the report into an interview.

## Handoff gate

A bet is ready for handoff when all of these are true:

- The user selected this bet or explicitly asked to package it for selection.
- Its motive and success conditions are clear. Success may be an experience, learning, an artifact, practical utility, distinctiveness, external adoption, profit, or a stated combination.
- The intended participants, beneficiaries, or recipients are clear when they matter.
- Its rationale, accepted scope, and non-goals define a coherent commitment.
- Existing approaches received a check proportionate to the decision and the role they play.
- Claims about distinctiveness, demand, or profit have evidence proportionate to their importance.
- Material assumptions are supported, assigned to a bounded experiment, or accepted as risk.
- Appetite and constraints are sufficient to guide planning.
- Open questions fit the next activity rather than hiding unresolved purpose or boundary questions.
- The bet document distinguishes user decisions, evidence, and recommendations.

If a failed item could change why, whether, or what to pursue, keep incubating. Otherwise recommend the next fitting activity, which may be practice, adoption, experimentation, planning, building, or commercial validation. Readiness never selects a bet for the user.
